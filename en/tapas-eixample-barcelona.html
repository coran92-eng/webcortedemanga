Corte de Manga / Eating tapas in the Eixample

Eating tapas
in the Eixample

A practical guide to the neighbourhood, written from behind the bar: when people actually eat, what you should expect to spend, what to order first, and the mistakes we watch visitors make every week.

The timing catches everyone out

If you're visiting, this is the first thing to know: nobody in Barcelona has dinner at 7pm. Most Eixample kitchens open around 13:00 for lunch and around 20:00 for dinner, and plenty stop serving food between 22:30 and 23:00 even though the bar stays open much later.

Hence the classic misunderstanding: you see a busy bar at midnight, walk in for dinner, and are told the kitchen closed hours ago. Those people are drinking, not eating.

Practical rule: if you want dinner, be seated by 22:30. If you want a drink afterwards, you have plenty of time.

In our case: the kitchen runs 08:00 to 23:00 every day, and we stay open until 01:00 for drinks and cocktails.

What it costs

The Eixample has everything from neighbourhood bars to Michelin dining rooms. For a mid-range tapas gastrobar — the most common thing around here — expect roughly:

  • Small bites (gildas, olives, bread with tomato): €3–6
  • Cooked tapas (croquettes, patatas bravas, omelettes, ensaladilla): €7–13
  • Stews and meat dishes (pork cheek, artichokes): €15–20
  • Premium produce (hand-carved ham, tinned seafood): €20–25
  • Signature cocktails: €10–12

In plain terms: four or five dishes shared between two people is plenty. With drinks, the bill usually lands between €25 and €40 per person.

If you'd rather have a fixed price, ask about group menus. Ours run from €32 to €48 per person for groups of 4 to 15.

How to order: share, and in rounds

The most common mistake is ordering one dish per person like a regular restaurant. That's not how it works. Tapas go in the middle of the table and everyone eats from them.

What works best:

  • Start with two or three small bites while you decide (gildas, bread with tomato, a tin of something).
  • Order a first round of three or four cooked tapas.
  • Finish that round before ordering the next. Everything arrives hot and you don't over- or under-order.

And one thing any bar will thank you for: mention allergies and intolerances at the start, not once the plate is on the table.

What to order on a first visit

Three or four dishes tell you whether a tapas kitchen is being run properly:

  • The tortilla. Made to order rather than sitting on the counter for hours — good sign.
  • The croquettes. If they're handmade you can tell from the béchamel: creamy, not a frozen brick.
  • The patatas bravas. Every house has its own sauce; what matters is that the potato was fried just now.
  • A stew. Pork cheek, oxtail, tripe. This is the one you can't fake.

From our own menu, the three that leave the kitchen most are the confit Tudela artichokes with Iberian ham and egg yolk (€19), the pork cheek braised in red wine (€17.90) and the Burrata Secchiello. You can see the full menu with prices.

Book or walk in?

Sunday to Wednesday you'll almost always find a table without booking. Thursday to Saturday evening, you won't. If there are more than two of you, or you want a specific time, book.

If you're a group, book — and give us the real headcount. Turning up ten-strong with no warning means someone gets left standing or we have to split you across two tables.

The area around Urgell

The Esquerra de l'Eixample, around Comte d'Urgell, is a less touristy part of the district than the streets near Passeig de Gràcia. It's mostly where people who live and work here eat, which in practice means more sensible prices and fewer laminated menus with photographs on them.

Easy to reach: metro Urgell (L1) and Hospital Clínic (L5), plus buses 9, 27, 54 and 56. About 15 minutes on foot from Plaça Catalunya.
